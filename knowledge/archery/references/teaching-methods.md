# Teaching methods — pick the method for the target

The **Aim** stage. A method is chosen by what the learner must be able to do afterwards. It's not chosen by habit or by what's fashionable.

**Provenance:** the seven methods below, with their steps, advantages and limits, come from a Thai guide (kru2day, 2017) that draws on Tisana Khammani (2000). The choose-by-verb table and the slide notes are this skill's own assessment.

---

## 1. Choose by target verb

| The learner must be able to… | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow or perform a procedure; operate equipment | **Demonstration** | they need to see the steps done correctly once |
| Retell a sequence or story memorably; practise speaking | **Dramatization** | acting fixes a sequence in memory |
| Handle a live situation involving people; understand another's view | **Role playing** | it builds empathy and decision-making under pressure |
| Weigh options when there is no single right answer | **Case study** | it exposes the reasons behind answers |
| Practise a skill or recall facts with high participation | **Game** | fun and direct experience |
| Understand a complex system through consequences | **Simulation** | decisions have effects, as in the real system |
| Form a definition or category ("what makes X an X") | **Concept attainment** | learners discover the defining features |
| Simply receive information | a short explanation with one example | don't force a method onto a fact |

**Every method in the source ends the same way:** discussion, then summary. That is where the learning is consolidated. A game with no debrief is entertainment.

## 2. The seven methods

### Demonstration

The teacher, a student, a group or a guest shows how something is done.

| Phase | Do |
| --- | --- |
| Prepare | state the purpose, order the steps, prepare materials and questions, set the timing, arrange the room so everyone can see, **rehearse it once** |
| Demonstrate | tell learners the purpose and what they must do (note, observe, summarise, answer), then run the steps in order with clear examples |
| Close | the teacher or the learners summarise, then check understanding by questions, a report or a repeat performance, and leave time for questions |

Use it when equipment is expensive or dangerous, when the concept is easier seen than said, and as a hook at the start of a lesson. Variants: whole class, small group, individual (for those who didn't get it).
**Limit:** it's teacher-centred, and learners participate little unless you build it in.
**Slides:** one step per slide with a photo or diagram, numbered. Don't narrate over a wall of text.

### Dramatization

Learners act out a prepared story, then discuss it.

**Steps:** prepare or choose the script (learners help choose) → assign roles (willing volunteers) → rehearse → perform and watch (no interrupting; tell the audience what to look for) → discuss the story and how convincingly it was played.
**Advantages:** real experience, participation, speaking, writing and teamwork.
**Limits:** takes time and money, and needs someone who can write a script.
**Slides:** the brief and the timings only. The stage is the classroom.

### Role playing

Learners take roles in a realistic situation **with no script**, then discuss what happened.

**Steps:** present the situation and roles (it can be a problem or conflict to resolve) → choose players (willing ones; sometimes deliberately against type for the experience) → brief the observers on what to watch, with an observation sheet if useful → play → discuss knowledge, thoughts, feelings and behaviour.
**Advantages:** empathy, attitude change, decision-making.
**Limits:** takes time, and the teacher must handle unexpected turns.
**Slides:** the scenario card and the observer's questions. No answers.

### Case study

Learners read a realistic problem case, answer the questions, and discuss reasons.

**Steps:** present the case (a conflict or dilemma, real or invented) → small groups study it and **don't answer immediately** → each learner prepares an answer first, then the group discusses → groups report → the class discusses reasons and summarises.
**Point:** there's no clearly right or wrong answer. The aim is to see varied answers and, above all, **the reasoning behind them**.
**Limit:** the case isn't happening to the learner, so answers tend toward what "should" be done, not what would be done.
**Slides:** the case on one slide, the question on the next. Never put the "answer" on a slide.

### Game

Learners play by the rules, then use the play as material for discussion.

**Steps:** explain the rules → play while the teacher observes and notes → discuss the results and play behaviour, tied to the objective. Three educational game types: non-competitive, competitive, and simulation games. Purposes: practise a skill, learn content, or learn how a situation works.
**Limit:** the debrief needs a skilled facilitator, or the learning evaporates.
**Slides:** the rules and a timer. Keep rules to what fits on one slide.

### Simulation

Learners play roles in a situation with realistic information and rules, and their decisions have realistic consequences.

**Steps:** prepare → present the reason and the big picture **before** details → assign roles (everyone gets one) → play by the rules while the teacher coaches → discuss what influenced the outcomes → summarise.
**Types:** a pure simulation, or a game-style one that reflects reality (an ordinary game doesn't).
**Limits:** costly, and needs heavy preparation. If no simulation exists, you must build one.
**Slides:** the situation, role cards, and a state board updated after each round.

### Concept attainment

Learners discover a concept by comparing examples that fit it against examples that don't, then define it themselves.

**Steps:** prepare two sets of examples (fit and don't fit) → explain the rules → present examples → learners name the defining features → learners define the concept.

Four ways to present, and each trades speed against thinking:

| Presentation | Speed | Thinking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All the "yes" examples, then all the "no" examples | fastest | least |
| "Yes" and "no" alternated | slower | more |
| One of each, then learners judge the rest and you confirm | slower | learners test hypotheses |
| One of each, then learners **propose** examples and you confirm | slowest | the most |

**Slides:** a two-column yes/no layout, revealed one example at a time.

## When not to use a method

- **The target is only information.** Give a short explanation and an example, then check with one question.
- **You can't afford the debrief.** Skip the game or the role play. A method run without its discussion phase is worse than a plain explanation.
- **The class has no shared prerequisite.** Simulations and cases assume knowledge to reason with. Teach the basics first.
